# Writing for the interface

Read when you write any visible text: labels, buttons, headings, hints, errors, empty states, instructions. This app's glossary and EN/VI rules are in app-notes.md.
- Text is part of the interface. Design with real content, never placeholder text. `[Myth: content isn't needed to design]`
- People scan. They read under 20% of a page, and concise, scannable, objective copy tested 124% better. Use meaningful headings, put key words first, keep paragraphs short, use lists, and cut promotion, small talk and needless instructions. `[Myth: people read on the web]`
- Use the words people already use and look for. They take the first plausible match, so labels must match their wording. `[Mental model]` `[Myth: users make optimal choices]`
- Use one word for one thing everywhere, so people can build a model of the product they can trust. `[Mental model]` `[Jakob]`
- Others can't see what's in your head. State requirements, define terms, and don't assume a label or step is obvious. (Tappers expected listeners to name half their songs; listeners named 3%.) `[Illusion of transparency]`
- Give icons a text label. Only widely known icons, like close, play or print, can stand alone. `[Myth: icons enhance usability]`
- Treat microcopy as part of the design: labels, hints and error messages change outcomes and are worth testing. `[Myth: small details don't matter]`
- Say what happens next; people avoid steps whose outcome is unclear. `[Ambiguity effect]`
- Word options evenly, with no guilt or fear. `[Framing]`
- Words set up what follows: goal and achievement wording motivates. Avoid wording that reinforces stereotypes. `[Priming]`
- Keep chunks short and grouped. Working memory holds about 4 to 7 chunks, each for 20 to 30 seconds. `[Working memory]` `[Chunking]`
