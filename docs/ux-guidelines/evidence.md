# Evidence

The source behind every tag in the guide. Read only to justify a rule, weigh two rules against each other, or decide a case no rule covers; search for the tag. Each entry gives the finding, where it comes from, and a link. Some UX Myths sources are dated (2008–2014) or anecdotal, so weigh them below the lab findings.

## Laws of UX

- `[Aesthetic-usability]` Attractive designs are judged easier to use and their flaws forgiven, which can hide problems in testing. Kurosu & Kashimura 1995: 26 ATM layouts, 252 people; looks predicted perceived ease better than actual ease. [source](https://lawsofux.com/aesthetic-usability-effect/)
- `[Chunking]` Information grouped into meaningful units is easier to scan, understand and remember. Miller 1956. [source](https://lawsofux.com/chunking/)
- `[Cognitive bias]` Mental shortcuts cause systematic errors in judgment; knowing about them helps you spot them but doesn't remove them. Tversky & Kahneman 1972. [source](https://lawsofux.com/cognitive-bias/)
- `[Cognitive load]` When information exceeds mental capacity, people miss details and feel overwhelmed; elements that don't aid understanding add load. Sweller 1988. [source](https://lawsofux.com/cognitive-load/)
- `[Common region]` Items inside a shared border or background are seen as a group. Gestalt. [source](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-common-region/)
- `[Doherty]` Productivity peaks when the system responds within 400 ms (Doherty & Thadani 1982, down from 2 s). Perceived performance, animation while loading and progress bars make waits feel shorter; a deliberate delay can raise trust. [source](https://lawsofux.com/doherty-threshold/)
- `[Fitts]` Time to reach a target grows with distance and shrinks with size; fast moves to small targets cause errors. Fitts 1954. [source](https://lawsofux.com/fittss-law/)
- `[Flow]` People are absorbed when challenge matches skill, with feedback on progress and little friction; too hard frustrates, too easy bores. Csíkszentmihályi 1975. [source](https://lawsofux.com/flow/)
- `[Goal-gradient]` Effort rises near a goal; clear progress indicators, and progress already given, increase completion. Hull 1932. [source](https://lawsofux.com/goal-gradient-effect/)
- `[Hick]` Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. Hick & Hyman 1952. Limit options when speed matters, highlight a recommendation, split complex tasks, but don't oversimplify into vagueness. [source](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)
- `[Jakob]` People expect your product to work like the ones they use most; when changing a design, let people keep the familiar version for a while. Nielsen. [source](https://lawsofux.com/jakobs-law/)
- `[Mental model]` People act on their own model of how a system works and transfer it to similar products; match it. Craik 1943. [source](https://lawsofux.com/mental-model/)
- `[Miller]` Working memory holds about 7 ± 2 items, varying by person and context; don't use "7" to justify needless limits, chunk instead. Miller 1956. [source](https://lawsofux.com/millers-law/)
- `[Occam]` Prefer the option with the fewest assumptions; remove elements until removing more would hurt. William of Ockham. [source](https://lawsofux.com/occams-razor/)
- `[Paradox of the active user]` New users skip manuals and start using the product, even when that causes errors; put help in context. Rosson & Carroll 1987. [source](https://lawsofux.com/paradox-of-the-active-user/)
- `[Pareto]` Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes; focus effort where it helps the most people. Pareto. [source](https://lawsofux.com/pareto-principle/)
- `[Parkinson]` A task expands to fill the time available; finish faster than people expect, for example with autofill. Parkinson 1955. [source](https://lawsofux.com/parkinsons-law/)
- `[Peak-end]` Experiences are judged by their peak and their end, and bad moments are remembered more vividly. Kahneman et al. 1993: people preferred to repeat a longer cold-water trial that ended slightly warmer. [source](https://lawsofux.com/peak-end-rule/)
- `[Postel]` Be liberal in what you accept and conservative in what you send: accept varied input, convert it, set limits, give clear feedback. Postel. [source](https://lawsofux.com/postels-law/)
- `[Prägnanz]` People read complex or ambiguous shapes as the simplest form; simple figures are processed and remembered more easily. Wertheimer 1910. [source](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-pr%C3%A4gnanz/)
- `[Proximity]` Things near each other are seen as related and are understood faster. Gestalt. [source](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-proximity/)
- `[Selective attention]` People filter for what fits their goal; they ignore anything that looks like an ad (banner blindness) and miss changes made at the same time as others (change blindness). Broadbent 1958. [source](https://lawsofux.com/selective-attention/)
- `[Serial position]` First and last items in a series are recalled best; put key items at the ends, the least important in the middle. Ebbinghaus. [source](https://lawsofux.com/serial-position-effect/)
- `[Similarity]` Elements that look alike are seen as related; links and navigation should look different from text. Gestalt. [source](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-similarity/)
- `[Tesler]` Every process has complexity that can't be removed, only moved; the system should absorb as much as possible. Tesler, Xerox PARC, 1980s. [source](https://lawsofux.com/teslers-law/)
- `[Uniform connectedness]` Visually connected elements (shared colour, line, frame) are seen as more related than unconnected ones. Gestalt. [source](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-uniform-connectedness/)
- `[Von Restorff]` The item that differs is the one remembered; use emphasis sparingly, not by colour alone, and be careful with motion. von Restorff 1933. [source](https://lawsofux.com/von-restorff-effect/)
- `[Working memory]` Holds about 4–7 chunks, each fading in 20–30 s; recognition beats recall, so show visited states and carry information across screens. [source](https://lawsofux.com/working-memory/)
- `[Zeigarnik]` Unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones; signal more content and show progress. Zeigarnik 1920s. [source](https://lawsofux.com/zeigarnik-effect/)

## UX Myths

- `[Myth: people read on the web]` Under 20% of a page's text is read (Nielsen 2008); concise, scannable copy tested 124% better. [#1](https://uxmyths.com/post/647473628/myth-people-read-on-the-web)
- `[Myth: 3 clicks]` Click count doesn't affect success or satisfaction; clear labels do. Moving products from 3 to 4 clicks deep raised findability 600% in one test. [#2](https://uxmyths.com/post/654026581/myth-all-pages-should-be-accessible-in-3-clicks)
- `[Myth: people don't scroll]` People scrolled on 76% of pages (ClickTale); 90% of mobile users scroll within 14 s; the top still gets the most attention. [#3](https://uxmyths.com/post/654047943/myth-people-dont-scroll)
- `[Myth: design is about looks]` Design is how it works: balance form and function, and base it on research and testing. [#4](https://uxmyths.com/post/654070104/myth-design-is-about-making-a-website-look-good)
- `[Myth: accessibility is expensive]` Built in from the start it costs about the same as an inaccessible build, and is cheaper to maintain. [#5](https://uxmyths.com/post/654091803/myth-accessibility-is-expensive-and-difficult)
- `[Myth: accessible sites are ugly]` Separating content from styling keeps visual design free. [#6](https://uxmyths.com/post/702066202/myth-6-accessible-sites-are-ugly)
- `[Myth: graphics make things visible]` Eye-tracking shows people skip anything that looks like an ad, even urgent health instructions; contrast and expected placement work. [#7](https://uxmyths.com/post/702072231/myth-graphics-will-make-a-page-element-more-visible)
- `[Myth: stock photos help]` Decorative images are looked at less and ignored; informational images help. [#8](https://uxmyths.com/post/705397950/myth-ornamental-graphics-improves-the-users-experience)
- `[Myth: design has to be original]` Conventions need no instructions; depart only when sure the alternative is better. [#9](https://uxmyths.com/post/712377283/myth-9-design-has-to-be-original)
- `[Myth: small details don't matter]` An "optional" label doubled sign-ups (40% → 80%); skipping sign-up added about $300 M a year; better error messages saved over £250,000 a year. [#10](https://uxmyths.com/post/712451083/myth-if-your-design-is-good-small-details-dont-matter)
- `[Myth: redesign periodically]` Big-bang redesigns upset users; redesign to fix real problems, in small steps. [#11](https://uxmyths.com/post/712537920/myth-you-need-to-redesign-your-website-periodically)
- `[Myth: more choices and features]` Fewer options raised buying and satisfaction; after use, usability beats feature count, and features are hard to remove. [#12](https://uxmyths.com/post/712569752/myth-more-choices-and-features-result-in-higher-satisfac)
- `[Myth: icons enhance usability]` Few icons have a shared meaning; text labels fixed Outlook's toolbar; people remember button position more than the picture. [#13](https://uxmyths.com/post/715009009/myth-icons-enhance-usability)
- `[Myth: you are like your users]` Experts can't see with beginners' eyes (curse of knowledge); design for outsiders and test with them. [#14](https://uxmyths.com/post/715988395/myth-you-are-like-your-users)
- `[Myth: users make optimal choices]` People pick the first reasonable option (satisficing), not the best one. [#15](https://uxmyths.com/post/717722982/myth-15-users-make-optimal-choices)
- `[Myth: search fixes navigation]` 70% started tasks with a link, 30% with search; no users were search-dominant. [#16](https://uxmyths.com/post/717755413/myth-search-will-solve-a-websites-navigation-problems)
- `[Myth: the homepage matters most]` One site's homepage share fell from 39% to 19% of views; deep pages get most visits. [#17](https://uxmyths.com/post/717779908/myth-the-homepage-is-your-most-important-page)
- `[Myth: content isn't needed to design]` Good content in a simple page beats a nice layout with weak text; design with real content. [#19](https://uxmyths.com/post/718187422/myth-you-dont-need-the-content-to-design-a-website)
- `[Myth: if it works for Amazon]` Same software, same sales: Amazon got 1,805 reviews, Target 3; context decides what works. [#20](https://uxmyths.com/post/718217318/myth-if-it-works-for-amazon-it-will-work-for-you)
- `[Myth: people can tell you what they want]` Only 25–27% like the dark roast they say they want; Walmart lost $1.85 B removing aisles at customers' request. Observe behaviour. [#21](https://uxmyths.com/post/746610684/myth-21-people-can-tell-you-what-they-want)
- `[Myth: testing is expensive]` 3–5 users reveal most big problems; paper prototypes and guerrilla recruiting are enough. [#22](https://uxmyths.com/post/831431504/myth-22-usability-testing-is-expensive)
- `[Myth: 7±2]` Miller's limit is about recall, not visible options; broad, shallow menus often work better than deep ones. [#23](https://uxmyths.com/post/931925744/myth-23-choices-should-always-be-limited-to-seven)
- `[Myth: people use it as you imagined]` Kleenex sales doubled once marketed for how people really used it; watch real use. [#24](https://uxmyths.com/post/1048425031/myth-24-people-always-use-your-product-the-way-you-imagi)
- `[Myth: aesthetics don't matter]` 46.1% of 2,500+ people judged credibility partly by visual design. [#25](https://uxmyths.com/post/1161244116/myth-25-aesthetics-are-not-important-if-you-have-good-us)
- `[Myth: testing = focus groups]` Focus groups capture what people say; usability tests show what they do. [#26](https://uxmyths.com/post/1319999199/myth-26-usability-testing-focus-groups)
- `[Myth: UX is about usability]` Good UX also evokes positive emotion, not only the absence of frustration. [#27](https://uxmyths.com/post/1533970267/myth-27-ux-design-is-about-usability)
- `[Myth: white space is wasted]` White space aids comprehension and sets priority. [#28](https://uxmyths.com/post/2059998441/myth-28-white-space-is-wasted-space)
- `[Myth: people are rational]` A decoy doubled sales of the pricier option; anchors and "free" shift choices predictably. [#29](https://uxmyths.com/post/2607991907/myth-29-people-are-rational)
- `[Myth: experts don't need to test]` Experts predict user reactions poorly; combine expert review with user tests. [#30](https://uxmyths.com/post/3086989914/myth-30-if-you-are-expert-you-dont-need-to-test-your-des)
- `[Myth: UX is a step]` UX runs from strategy and research through development and after launch. [#31](https://uxmyths.com/post/3897350094/myth-31-ux-design-is-a-step-in-a-project)
- `[Myth: success happens overnight]` The iPod took about three years to take off; plan for iteration. [#32](https://uxmyths.com/post/4956005041/myth-32-success-happens-overnight)
- `[Myth: mobile users are distracted]` 60% of phone use is at home; people multitask more on a PC (67%) than on a phone (57%). Data from 2012–14. [#33](https://uxmyths.com/post/99302792550/myth-33-mobile-users-are-distracted)
- `[Myth: simple = minimal]` Hiding labels and functions adds effort; simplicity is less effort, not fewer elements. [#34](https://uxmyths.com/post/115783813605/myth-34-simplicity-minimalism)

## The Decision Lab

- `[Accountability diffusion]` When AI shapes decisions, responsibility spreads until no one feels answerable; name one decision owner. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/accountability-diffusion-in-ai)
- `[AI literacy gap]` People feel confident with AI they understand poorly; show uncertainty, put checks near high-stakes output, treat overrides as healthy. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/ai-literacy-gap)
- `[Ambiguity effect]` People avoid options with unknown odds (Ellsberg 1961); complete information makes options feel less risky. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/ambiguity-effect)
- `[Anchoring]` The first number seen pulls judgment: judges gave 28.7 months after a 34-month demand vs 18.8 after a 2-month one. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/anchoring-bias)
- `[Authority bias]` People defer to authority cues; 65% in Milgram 1961 went to the maximum shock when told to. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/authority-bias)
- `[Automation bias]` People follow automated advice against evidence: 59% accuracy with an aid vs 97% without (Mosier & Skitka, 1999). Visible reasoning, warnings, cheap overrides and audits help. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/automation-bias)
- `[Base rate]` Vivid specifics beat statistics: with a 1-in-1,000 disease and 5% false positives, the right answer is 2%, but the average answer was 56%. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/base-rate-fallacy)
- `[Choice overload]` 30% used a coupon after 6 jams vs 3% after 24 (Iyengar & Lepper); experts are less affected; compare side by side, filter, curate. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/choice-overload-bias) · [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/choice-overload/)
- `[Confirmation bias]` People seek and trust evidence that fits their beliefs; look for disconfirming evidence. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/confirmation-bias)
- `[Decision fatigue]` Decision quality drops after many decisions: parole was granted about 70% early in the day, about 10 points less later. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/decision-fatigue)
- `[Decoy]` A weaker third option shifts choice: 84% picked print + web with a decoy vs 32% without. Use only to help people find what fits. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/decoy-effect)
- `[Delegation creep]` People steadily widen what automated systems may do; tier tasks by stakes, set red lines, audit actual use. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/delegation-creep)
- `[False consensus]` People overestimate how many share their views and habits; test with diverse real users. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/false-consensus-effect)
- `[Framing]` The same choice framed as 200 saved got 72% support, as 400 lost 22% (Tversky & Kahneman 1981). Present balanced frames; avoid guilt-based messages. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/framing-effect)
- `[Halo effect]` One strong impression colours unrelated qualities: other ratings explained almost 50% of hotel "location" ratings. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/halo-effect)
- `[Hyperbolic discounting]` People prefer smaller rewards now over larger ones later; make future benefits concrete. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/hyperbolic-discounting)
- `[IKEA effect]` People value what they built (Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012), but not when they failed to finish. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/ikea-effect)
- `[Illusion of transparency]` People overestimate how well others read their minds: tappers expected 50% of songs to be named, listeners named 3% (Newton 1990). [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/illusion-of-transparency)
- `[Loss aversion]` Losses feel about twice as strong as equal gains; a 10% price rise cut demand 7.8%, a 10% cut raised it 3.3%. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/loss-aversion)
- `[Mere exposure]` Familiarity breeds liking; use standard layouts and consistent branding. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/mere-exposure-effect)
- `[Negativity bias]` Negative events weigh more than positive ones of the same size. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/negativity-bias)
- `[Observer expectancy]` Observers' expectations change results (Rosenthal & Fode 1963); limit cues to participants. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/observer-expectancy-effect)
- `[Parasocial trust in AI]` Human-like AI invites oversharing and unchecked compliance; make its non-human nature clear and add human review to high-stakes steps. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/parasocial-trust-in-ai)
- `[Planning fallacy]` People underestimate time: only 30% of students finished their thesis by the date they predicted (Buehler et al. 1994). Use past durations and smaller parts. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/planning-fallacy)
- `[Priming]` Earlier words shape later behaviour without awareness: achievement words in a CEO email lifted effectiveness 15%. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/priming)
- `[Regret aversion]` Fear of regret steers choices; don't exploit it with false urgency, show realistic outcomes. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/regret-aversion)
- `[Response bias]` People misreport in surveys; use neutral wording, middle options and short surveys. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/response-bias)
- `[Salience]` People focus on what stands out; show costs and consequences at the moment of decision. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/salience-bias)
- `[Social norms]` Seeing others act sways people: 6% followed a sign, 49% with one role model, 67% with two. Don't manufacture obligation. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/social-norms)
- `[Status quo]` People stick with the default even when it's no better (Samuelson & Zeckhauser 1988); make defaults serve the user. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/status-quo-bias)
- `[Survivorship]` Looking only at survivors hides failures and inflates results; check the denominator and what dropped out. [source](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/survivorship-bias)
