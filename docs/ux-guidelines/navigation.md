# Navigation and wayfinding

Read when you add or change menus, links, tabs, page names or search, or move something people already know.

- Follow common conventions for navigation, links and controls; people bring what they know from other products. Depart only when you are sure the alternative works better. `[Jakob]` `[Mental model]` `[Myth: design has to be original]`
- Keep navigation and controls consistent across screens; familiar layouts feel easy. `[Mere exposure]`
- Name every page, tab and link with the words people would look for. People take the first plausible option, so the right one must look right at a glance. `[Myth: users make optimal choices]`
- Each click should take people visibly closer to their goal. Knowing they're on the right track matters more than the number of clicks. `[Myth: 3 clicks]`
- Build clear menus and links first. Search is for known items and for when people get stuck; it doesn't fix unclear navigation. `[Myth: search fixes navigation]`
- Show where people are and where they've been (current item highlighted, visited links, breadcrumbs). Recognising is easier than remembering. `[Working memory]`
- Keep nav items in fixed places with text labels. People find controls by position, and few icons are understood without a word. `[Myth: icons enhance usability]`
- Put the most important destinations first and last in a nav. `[Serial position]`
- People often land on deep pages from links and search, so every page says what it is and how to get elsewhere. `[Myth: the homepage matters most]`
- Give phone users the same features; most phone use happens at home or at work, not on the move. `[Myth: mobile users are distracted]`
- Redesign only to solve a real problem, in small steps. When a change is large, let people keep the old version for a while. `[Myth: redesign periodically]` `[Jakob]`
