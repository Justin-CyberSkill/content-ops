# Layout and visual hierarchy

Read when you arrange a screen: grouping, what stands out, images, spacing, long pages.

## Grouping

- Put related things close together and unrelated things further apart. Spacing is the first grouping tool. `[Proximity]`
- When spacing isn't enough, mark a group with a shared border or background. `[Common region]`
- Things that look alike are read as alike. Give items with the same function the same look, and make links and actions look different from plain text. `[Similarity]`
- Tie related items together with a shared frame, line or colour to show they belong together or follow on. `[Uniform connectedness]`
- Prefer simple, regular shapes and layouts. People read complex ones as their simplest form and remember simple ones better. `[Prägnanz]`
- Break content into distinct groups with a clear hierarchy so people can scan to the part that serves their goal. `[Chunking]`

## Emphasis

- Give the most important action or fact a distinct look, and keep emphasis rare so highlights don't compete. `[Von Restorff]`
- People ignore anything that looks like an ad: banners, flashy graphics, ad-like spots. Use contrast and expected placement for important content instead. `[Selective attention]` `[Myth: graphics make things visible]`
- When several things change at once, people miss some. Make each important change noticeable on its own. `[Selective attention]`
- People weigh what stands out most. Make sure that is what matters at that moment, such as a cost or consequence. `[Salience]`

## Images and polish

- Use images that inform or help people find their way. Drop decorative and stock images; people skip them. `[Myth: stock photos help]`
- Visual design is part of usability: it shapes first impressions and trust (46% judged credibility partly by looks). But looks also hide usability problems, so judge the flow, not the look. `[Myth: aesthetics don't matter]` `[Aesthetic-usability]`
- One weak detail colours how people judge the rest. Leave no part noticeably worse than the others. `[Halo effect]`

## Space, length and order

- White space is structure: it groups, sets priority and helps comprehension. Don't fill it for its own sake. `[Myth: white space is wasted]`
- People scroll. Don't cram everything into the first screen, but put what the page is and its main action at the top, and let a cut-off edge show there is more. `[Myth: people don't scroll]`
- Remove every element that doesn't help the task; a screen is done when nothing else can go without hurting it. `[Occam]` `[Cognitive load]`
- Put the most used items at the start and end of a list or row; the middle is remembered least. `[Serial position]`
- Spend the most effort on the few screens and flows people use most, and on deep pages where people actually arrive. `[Pareto]` `[Myth: the homepage matters most]`
